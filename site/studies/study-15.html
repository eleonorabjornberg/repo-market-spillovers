Study 15: corporate bonds in repo, and in a stock-market panic — result

Design: docs/design-study-15-corporate-bonds.md, committed before the data was fetched, with two amendments. The second was written after a first result was seen, which turned out to be a data artefact; the amendment says so. Every figure is read from result.json.

In one line

Corporate bonds are a small part of repo: about 5% of dealers' repo against corporate bonds and Treasuries. They are not where the leverage is. They matter for a different reason. On stock-market panic days when Treasuries fail as a haven, corporate bond yields rose too on 89% of days [83, 94]. Since 2020 that case has become common, so holders of corporate bonds have had no offset just when they would need one.

1. How much repo rests on corporate bonds? Not much (verdict: small; grew faster than Treasury repo)

Primary dealers' repo against corporate debt and against Treasuries, from the New York Fed's weekly totals. The corporate totals agree with the OFR's copy of the same data within 1% in 99.7% of the weeks of 2015–2021.

Shares are of corporate plus Treasury. Corporate repo grew 138% against Treasury repo's 94%, so it grew faster, from a small base. Most of it is dealers borrowing cash against their own corporate bond holdings: in the latest weeks about 70% of that sat in tri-party repo (the venue split exists only from July 2024). Dealers lend little cash against corporate bonds: 1.6% of their reverse repo, the rest against Treasuries.

2. Did lenders pull back from corporate collateral in March 2020? No (verdict: not supported)

Change from the four weeks to 26 February 2020 to the four weeks from 18 March 2020:

CorporateTreasury
Dealers' repo (borrowing cash)+11.9%+11.6%
Dealers' reverse repo (lending cash)+18.7%+16.5%

Dealers' corporate repo rose in March 2020, in step with their Treasury repo. But the second window opens the day after the Fed announced its Primary Dealer Credit Facility (17 March 2020). It lent to dealers against collateral that included investment-grade corporate bonds, and dealers' repo totals may include it. The data cannot separate private lenders from the Fed. So the honest reading is that lenders did not visibly run, and the Fed was standing behind the dealers when they might have.

April 2025 (four weeks to 2 April against four from 9 April, reported without a verdict): corporate repo was flat (−0.3%), while Treasury repo grew 9.3%.

Corporate bond yields are anchored to Treasury yields. When Treasuries rally in a panic, corporate yields fall too, though by about half as much, so even a rally leaves corporate bonds behind. When Treasuries sell off, corporate bonds sell off with them in nine panics out of ten. On 12 March 2020 the 10-year yield rose 6 bp and the Baa yield 27 bp. Since 2020, corporate bond yields have risen on nearly half of panic days, against fewer than one in five in 2008–19.

Financing against corporate securities fell by 42% from mid-2007 to the months after Lehman, 25 points more than against Treasuries, which fell 17% as dealers shrank. Mortgage-backed and agency financing held up, probably because the mortgage category in this format is mostly agency securities, backed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, which the government took into conservatorship on 7 September 2008. The data do not show the split. The run shows up where the collateral was private.

Compared with March 2020 (Q2), when dealers' corporate repo rose 12%, 2008 is the case where lenders pulled away from private collateral. Two cautions. First, "corporate securities" in the old format is not today's "corporate debt": it likely includes private-label mortgage and asset-backed securities, the collateral at the centre of 2008, so levels are not compared across formats. Second, "securities out" includes securities lending as well as repo. The Fed lent to dealers in 2008 as well (its dealer facility opened on 17 March 2008), so even these falls happened with support in place.

What this means for the question asked

If a crisis started outside repo, in a stock-market bust:

  • Repo's own exposure to corporate bonds is small. About $167bn of dealers' repo rests on corporate collateral, against about $3.4 trillion on Treasuries. A run on corporate collateral like the one on private collateral in 2008 would hit a small corner, not the core. In 2008 that corner shrank by 42% in just over a year while the rest held; today it is about 5% of dealers' corporate and Treasury repo.
  • Corporate bonds would not cushion a bust. Their yields go where Treasury yields go. When Treasuries are a haven, corporate bonds gain a little; when they are not, corporate bonds lose too. Study 09 found the second case has become more common since 2020, with inflation.
  • So the repo market's ability to cushion depends on Treasuries, as in studies 04 and 09. In March 2020 the Fed stood behind both Treasuries and dealers' corporate financing.

Limits

  • Dealers only, and their repo is partly their own choice: a rise can be dealers financing inventories they could not sell.
  • Two stress episodes in the repo data (2008 and March 2020), and both overlap the Fed's emergency lending.
  • Moody's Baa is an index of long, seasoned bonds, priced slowly. Q3 uses its yield level. Its spread over Treasuries widens mechanically when Treasury yields fall, which is why the spread is not the test.
  • Nothing here forecasts a crisis or says an AI bust is coming. It describes what happened in past panics.
  • 2008 uses an older report format (Q4): broader categories, repo and securities lending combined. It agrees with published research (Gorton and Metrick, 2012; Copeland, Martin and Walker, 2014) that the run on repo in 2008 hit private collateral hardest.

Cross-check against the published research (added 26 October 2026, after Q4)

Eleonora supplied both papers. Their findings, quoted:

  • Gorton and Metrick (NBER w15223, August 2009), on bilateral repo: their haircut index for repo collateral other than Treasuries "rises from zero in early 2007 to nearly 50 percent at the peak of the crisis in late 2008", and some asset classes "stopped entirely from being used as collateral".
  • Copeland, Martin and Walker (NY Fed Staff Report 506, revised August 2014), with confidential tri-party data from July 2008:
    • margins on Treasuries and agency debentures "did not change throughout the crisis";
    • corporate-bond collateral in tri-party fell by "about $80 billion, or roughly 44%" from July 2008 to April 2009, and private ABS by 66%;
    • they find "no system-wide run on repo", but "a sharp decline in the tri-party repo funding of Lehman".

Agreement. Their 44% fall in corporate collateral comes from different data, a different window and one segment of repo. It is close to Q4's 42% fall in dealers' financing against corporate securities. Both show the pullback concentrated in private collateral, with Treasuries spared.

A correction of wording. The limits above say "the run on repo in 2008". Copeland, Martin and Walker dispute that the repo market as a whole ran: in tri-party, lenders cut private collateral and pulled away from Lehman, while margins elsewhere barely moved. The accurate wording is that repo lenders pulled back from private collateral, and from the weakest dealer. The text above is left as written; this note governs.
