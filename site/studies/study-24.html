Study 24: can public data see the swap-spread trade? — result

Test, with a weak verdict. Design: docs/design-study-24-swap-spread-visibility.md, committed before the data was fetched. Every figure is read from result.json.

Sources: DTCC's public swap reports, 513 trading days from 7 November 2024 to 6 November 2026 (DTCC's files for 1–6 November 2024 have left its rolling window); the OFR Hedge Fund Monitor (Form PF, quarterly, to June 2026; the CFTC's leveraged-fund futures DV01, weekly); FRED DGS10. Each input's SHA-256 is in metadata/s24_manifest.json.

In one line

By the design's rules, a public measure of the swap-spread trade can be built from DTCC's swap reports: it passes both tests. But both passes are marginal. The Form PF correlation lands exactly on the threshold (0.6, on six quarters), and April 2025 is above its interval by 0.4 points. Read it as "public data can see this trade a little", not "public data tracks it".

The measure

A swap-spread trade puts one swap in a package with a Treasury (or a Treasury future). A swap-curve trade puts two or three swaps in one package. So package trades that carry a package spread are grouped by execution second, platform and spread. A group with one swap is a single-swap spread package, the candidate swap-spread trade. Risk is DV01: notional × annuity factor × 1 bp.

  • Single-swap spread packages are 14.2% of all new SOFR swap DV01 over the two years.
  • As a check on the inputs, the 10-year SOFR swap spread built from outright trades minus DGS10 is at plausible levels: −44.8 bp in the first week and −38.6 bp in the last, within −57.0 to −36.6 bp.

The front end grew most: single-swap spread DV01 at 1.5–4 years nearly tripled. That fits the Dallas Fed's account of front-end swap-spread wideners replacing the basis trade (McCormick, Ramaswamy and De Vere, 16 October 2026), and study 13's finding that the futures decline sits in the 2-year contract. It is descriptive, and its first and last three months were not chosen to test it. Over the same span leveraged funds' net futures DV01 went from −$475.6m to −$421.3m per bp.

What public data hides (Q5)

  • Direction. No trade says who paid fixed, so a new position and an unwind look the same. This is the main limit.
  • Size. 8.95% of SOFR swap DV01 is reported at a capped notional, so large trades are understated; for single-swap spread packages the share is 1.74%.
  • Structure. 24.46% of package trades carry no package spread and cannot be classified.
  • Counterparty. The prime-brokerage flag was FALSE on every trade seen in the design's check, so it cannot single out hedge funds.

Limits

  • Six quarters is too few for a strong test of Q1; one quarter's rank would move the correlation across the threshold.
  • Legs of one curve package executed in different seconds would be counted as single-swap packages.
  • The measure counts new trades of every user, not hedge funds alone.
  • One change after the design: the fetch first stopped on a malformed fixed rate. Rates outside −5% to 20% are now treated as missing (4% assumed), as the design already did for absent rates, and every day was re-fetched under the same rule.

What it means

The futures basis was visible every week. The trade that replaced it is visible only faintly: a measure can be built that moves with Form PF and caught the April 2025 unwind, but it carries no direction, and both of its passes are on the margin. Anyone relying on public data for crowding in the swap-spread trade should treat it as a rough flow gauge, not a position.
