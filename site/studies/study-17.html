Study 17: why 2025–26 month-ends are milder than 2018–19's, and what drove the slow creep — result

Design: docs/design-study-17-milder-spikes-and-creep.md, committed before any data was fetched. Section 3 holds checks added after the first results. They are labelled post hoc and decide nothing. Every figure is read from result.json. Data to 23 October 2026.

The spread here is SOFR minus the top of the Fed's target range, in bp. A month-end jump is the mean of the spread on the last business day of the month and the first of the next, minus its median on business days 6–15 of the month.

In one line

Ordinary month-ends were no milder in 2025–26 than in 2018–19. What changed is the extremes. Quarter-ends, and above all year-end 2018, spiked far higher before the Fed's standing repo facility existed. Since then, the worst turn day reached 22 bp above the facility's rate, against 65 bp in 2018–19: a ceiling that leaks but holds back the extremes. The slow creep from 2022 to 2025 goes mostly with the draining of the Fed's reverse repo facility, then of reserves.

A2, at equal tightness: scarcity accounts for it (the 2025–26 term is +0.0 bp [−5.0, +4.3]). Compared at the same ordinary-day tightness, 2025–26 jumps are the same size as 2018–19's. Both regimes covered the same range of ordinary-day spreads (−21 to −1 bp, and −21.5 to −5 bp). Within them, the size of the jump hardly depends on how tight ordinary days were (+0.13 per bp [−0.35, +0.54]).

Measured by reserves, the two regimes do not overlap. Reserves were 8.6–12.3% of bank assets in 2018–19 and 11.4–14.4% in 2025–26, so they cannot be compared at the same level of reserves. Repo reached the same tightness with noticeably more reserves in 2025–26. The level at which reserves become scarce has risen.

A4, clearing (descriptive): nothing visible. Within 2025–26, a higher sponsored share of cleared repo is not associated with smaller jumps: −0.11 bp per point [−1.08, +0.73], 17 months.

B1, the two drains: they account for the creep (82%). From 2022 to 2025 the spread rose 8.7 bp. The fitted model attributes 7.1 bp of it to the two drains: 4.8 bp to the fall in ON RRP and 2.3 bp to the fall in reserves. As the design warned, the split between the two is unreliable, because they trended together. Their combined account is the result. The order is clear from the table, though. Money funds' spare cash left the Fed's reverse repo facility first (2022–24), and only once it was nearly gone did falling reserves take over (2025–26).

B2, the 2018–19 relationship: not informative (post hoc judgement). The design fitted the spread on reserves over 2018–19 and predicted 2025–26. The fit has the wrong sign: more reserves, tighter repo (+0.97 bp per point). Its verdict ("looser than reserves predict", −4.7 bp [−8.2, −0.9]) should not be read. The design assumed that SOFR minus the top of the range is unaffected by the Fed's 2018–19 adjustments to the rate on reserves. That was wrong: the adjustments were made to pull market rates down within the range, and they did. So within 2018–19 the spread moved with policy as well as with reserves.

3. Post hoc: where the gap is (decides nothing)

The design dropped months in which the target range changed. In 2018 that removed three of the quarter-ends (June, September, December), because the Fed raised rates in those months. Keeping every month, which is safe for this measure because SOFR moves with the range:

2018–192025–26Difference
All month-ends (21 each)+10.1 bp+7.3 bp−2.9 [−9.7, +2.2]
Quarter-ends (7 each)+17.7 bp (median 14.0)+7.9 bp (median 8.0)−9.9 [−26.4, +2.1]
Other month-ends (14 each)+6.4 bp+7.0 bp+0.6 [−3.0, +4.6]

Ordinary month-ends are the same. The gap the one-pager shows sits in quarter-ends, and above all in one: year-end 2018, +62 bp. In 2018–19 SOFR reached 65 bp above the top of the range (2 January 2019). In 2025–26 the largest turn-day spread was 22 bp above it (31 October 2025). The 2018–19 quarter-ends are also mixed. September and December 2019 came after the Fed began its own repo operations, and December 2019 jumped only +0.5 bp.

Reading. This fits a ceiling more than anything else: the standing repo facility, in place since July 2021, removes the extreme spikes but leaks on reporting dates. Ordinary month-ends, which do not reach the ceiling, are as large as before. Less scarcity does not fit, because at equal tightness the jumps are the same. Clearing shows no effect within 2025–26. With 7 quarter-ends a side and one dominant outlier, none of this is a test. It is where to look.

For the one-pager. Chart A's 2018–19 month-end effect is probably lifted by year-end 2018. Checking it in repo-market-model without that date, or with quarter-ends apart, would show whether the "milder in 2025–26" reading holds. This project does not touch the parent model's results.

Limits

  • Two scarce regimes, about 20 months each, that differ in many ways at once.
  • One outlier drives the quarter-end gap. The year-end 2018 spike coincided with a stock-market sell-off and the Fed's December hike.
  • The facility series (RPONTSYD) counts all the Fed's overnight repo operations. In 2019 these were emergency operations, not the standing facility.
  • The design's comparability assumption failed for B2, as explained above. The same Fed adjustments also moved ordinary-day tightness B in 2019, so A2's comparison at equal tightness is less clean for 2019 than for 2018.
  • Monthly averages smooth over the days that matter.

Addendum (26 October 2026, post hoc): the facility's line before and after full allotment

Eleonora brought counts from another analysis of the standing repo facility's line, IORB + 10 bp, around the move to full allotment on 11 December 2025. All of Eleonora's figures reproduce exactly. Since full allotment, the facility has held the benchmark below its line, through October 2026's bond-market swings, though it went through at the 2025 year-end. With 0 of 203 days, the rate of breaches is at most about 1.5% of days at 95% confidence (the rule of three). It has not held the most expensive trades: the 99th percentile went above the line more often after full allotment than before. That is not what a firm ceiling would produce. A likely reason, not tested here, is that only some firms can borrow from the facility.

This refines section 3. "The worst turn since the facility, 22 bp above" (31 October 2025) predates full allotment.

Note on the addendum (26 October 2026): what full allotment was, and a confound. The New York Fed's "Statement Regarding Standing Overnight Repo Operations" (10 December 2025, effective 11 December) removed the facility's aggregate limit and moved it to a "full allotment format". Each eligible counterparty may submit one proposition per security type at each of the two daily operations, up to $40bn, awarded at the facility's rate. The implementation note of the same day set that rate at 3.75%, the top of the range, with IORB at 3.65%. The same FOMC decision also began reserve management purchases of Treasury bills, about $40bn in the first month from 12 December, to keep reserves ample. Two changes started at once, so the 2026 calm in the benchmark cannot be credited to full allotment alone. The design of the facility, eligible counterparties only and two fixed operation times a day, fits the reading that firms outside it still pay above its rate, but this note does not test that.
